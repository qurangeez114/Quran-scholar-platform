1. Earlier and late-antique textual witnesses

Dates refer to the approximate composition of works, not the lifetimes of Alexander or dates of modern translations. Earlier motifs are not necessarily the Qur’an’s direct sources.

c. AD 75–79 · Securely earlier than the Qur’an

Josephus — The Jewish War, 7.7.4

Josephus describes a mountain passage that, he says, Alexander had blocked with iron gates; the Alans later used it. This provides an early witness to an Alexander-and-iron-gates motif, but Josephus does not mention Gog and Magog or an iron-and-copper construction.

Passage: Josephus, The Jewish War, Book VII, chapter 7, §4 ↗ · Qur’an comparison: 18:96. The gate's actual construction by Alexander is not independently demonstrated by this notice.

Usually dated early 7th century, often c. AD 629–630 · Date and redactions debated

Syriac Alexander Legend — Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs

The legend explicitly names Alexander son of Philip, describes his divinely bestowed horns and journeys to the world's extremities, narrates people exposed to the rising sun, and has him construct an iron-and-brass gate against northern peoples including Gog and Magog. The gate’s destruction is also tied to divine intervention.

Verse comparisons: 18:83 (horned ruler); 18:86 (western waters); 18:90 (rising sun); 18:94 (Gog and Magog); 18:96–97 (barrier); 18:98 (its end).

Primary-text edition: E. A. Wallis Budge (1889), A Christian Legend concerning Alexander, within The History of Alexander the Great, vol. II; see especially pp. 151–154 for the gate. Read the translated text ↗ · Scholarly intertext index: Corpus Coranicum, 18:94 ↗.

Difference: The Syriac source names Alexander and contains extended Christian imperial prophecy; the Qur’an does not name Dhul-Qarnayn or reproduce that framework. Its composition may be contemporary with parts of the Qur’anic period, so priority and direct dependence are not established simply by textual resemblance.

3. Historical analysis: what changes—and what does not

What is documented

Long before modern debates, Alexander was associated with a northern iron gate. The Syriac legend shares a set of unusual motifs with Qur’an 18:83–98. Later Muslim commentators identified the Qur’anic figure differently and offered changing arguments for their views.

What is not proven

A direct textual line from Josephus or the Syriac legend into the Qur’an is not established by these parallels alone; the date of the Syriac text is disputed. Variation among commentators demonstrates development in interpretation, not by itself a change to the Qur’anic wording.

Editorial method: Each link goes to an actual text or source edition, with genre, date and degree of agreement noted. Scholars’ death dates approximate chronology; quotations are not treated as historical eyewitness reports. This dossier is a comparative research resource, not a claim of established borrowing or a historical verdict on Dhul-Qarnayn’s identity.
