## Dossier draft: Giving in secret, interest, and the distressed debtor

**Literary context.** Qur'an [2:271](https://quran.com/2/271) values disclosed charity but prefers concealment when giving to the poor. [Matthew 6:1–4](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/6) criticizes charitable performance to gain human praise and commends secret alms. Both associate discretion with divine recognition. **Difference:** the Qur'an explicitly says open giving is good, while Matthew's contrast targets conspicuous self-advertisement. This is a close ethical comparison, not a demonstration of direct dependence.

**Financial law.** [Qur'an 2:275](https://quran.com/2/275) distinguishes lawful trade from riba, and [2:278](https://quran.com/2/278) requires believers to abandon outstanding riba. [Leviticus 25:35–37](https://www.sefaria.org/Leviticus.25.35-37) forbids taking interest or profit from an impoverished brother. [Deuteronomy 23:19–20](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.23.19-20) prohibits charging a fellow Israelite interest while allowing it from a foreigner. **Difference:** these Torah laws specify social categories; the Qur'anic commands in this passage do not repeat Deuteronomy's foreigner exception. Neither the semantic range of Hebrew *neshekh/tarbit* nor Arabic *ribā* should be flattened into a modern universal interest-rate category without further philological study.

**Debtor hardship.** [Qur'an 2:280](https://quran.com/2/280) calls for postponement until an insolvent debtor can repay and commends forgiving the debt. [Exodus 22:25–27](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.22.24-26) restricts lending at interest to the poor and requires return of a garment pledged as security. Both protect vulnerable borrowers, but their concrete remedies differ. Note the Hebrew/English verse-numbering offset for Exodus 22:24–26 (Hebrew) versus 22:25–27 (common English Christian numbering).

**Chronology and witnesses.** The Torah's component legal traditions and final literary composition require distinct dating arguments; surviving medieval Masoretic codices, earlier Dead Sea Scroll witnesses where extant, and the Greek Septuagint cannot be conflated. Matthew is usually placed in the late first century CE, but composition date is not the date of surviving Greek papyri/codices. The Qur'an is seventh-century CE in historical context, and its manuscript witnesses/readings require separate description.
