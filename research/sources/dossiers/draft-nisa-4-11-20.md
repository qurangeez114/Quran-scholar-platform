# An-Nisāʾ 4:11–20 — unpublished legal-historical dossier

**Status:** preliminary, 2026-11-05. Not a public verified source dossier. No evidence of direct borrowing established.

## Chronological and legal setting

These verses form part of the Medinan Qur'anic treatment of inheritance, transgression, sexual misconduct, repentance, marriage and property. Biblical Pentateuchal law is a substantially earlier textual tradition, but the composition of individual legal strata and the dates of surviving manuscript witnesses are separate questions. Ancient Israelite inheritance practice is not a direct description of seventh-century Arabian practice.

## Coverage notes

4:12 treats spouses and collateral heirs, and requires a more precise primary-text succession comparison. 4:13–14 frame obedience and transgression without a specific external locator. 4:16–18 address misconduct and repentance; broad motifs alone are not publishable source associations. 4:19–20 concern women's marital agency, fair treatment and dower/property; further historically dated documentary evidence is needed. The lack of a staged passage is not proof no earlier parallel exists.

## Textual witnesses, interpretation, bibliography

Primary texts consulted in English: Numbers 27:8–11 via Sefaria source sheet; Numbers 27:8 Hebrew interlinear; Deuteronomy 22:22 via Bible Gateway comparison of translations. Edition-specific critical Hebrew readings, Septuagint variants, Dead Sea Scroll witnesses (if extant for relevant loci), and rabbinic interpretation remain uncollated. Later rabbinic texts must not automatically be treated as earlier than the Qur'an merely because they discuss Torah law. Future research should consider rabbinic inheritance discussion (e.g., Mishnah Bava Batra chapter 8) only after establishing passage text, redaction chronology and precise relevance.

Interpretive alternatives: common ancient legal problems, shared scriptural legal discourse, independent legal formulation, and possible mediated knowledge are competing explanations. A literary-dependence claim requires evidence beyond resemblance.
